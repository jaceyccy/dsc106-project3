Write-up

Motivation

Climate projections usually come as a single line per scenario, which hides two things: how long the two futures look alike, and how big the difference gets. We focus on the gap between a low- and a high-emissions future and let the reader decide which part of the century to look at.

Data and transformations

  • Source: CMIP6 on Google Cloud (Pangeo catalog), monthly near-surface air temperature (tas) from CESM2-WACCM, run r1i1p1f1, native grid (0.94° × 1.25°). The same run and grid are used for the historical, SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 experiments, so differences come from the scenario, not the model setup.
  • Region: a bounding box around California (32.5–42°N, 124.5–114°W), keeping only grid cells that are at least 50% land (64 of 88 cells). The box also covers most of Nevada and small parts of Oregon, Arizona and Baja California. Cells are weighted by cos(latitude) because cells near the poles cover less area. A global area-weighted mean is offered for comparison.
  • Time: monthly values are averaged to calendar years, weighted by days in each month, and converted from kelvin to °C. Window values are means of those annual values.
  • Baseline: the "vs 1995–2014" numbers compare against the same model's historical run for 1995–2014, the reference period the IPCC uses.
  • The "gap emerges" line: the first year after which the 11-year centered moving average of the gap never falls below +1 °C again (2057 for California, 2058 globally).
  • Limitation: this is one run of one model. Year-to-year wiggles are partly random weather inside the model, and other models would give somewhat different numbers.

Design rationale

  • Lines over time for the annual series, because position along a shared time axis is the most accurate way to compare two trends.
  • Color: blue for low emissions and orange for high, used the same way on every chart. We checked the pair for color-vision deficiency, and every color is also named in a direct label or legend, so color is never the only cue.
  • Window selection (brushing) as the main interaction: our exploratory graphs showed single years are noisy. The low-emissions run is warmer in 21 of 86 years, so a year slider would mislead. Averaging over a window the reader chooses shows the signal, and the presets give quick starting points.
  • Linked readouts and summary sentence: both window means, the gap, and the change since 1995–2014 update together, and the sentence compares the gap with ordinary year-to-year swings. That makes the main pattern, a gap that is small early on and large late, easy to find.
  • Annotation: the dashed "gap clearly emerges" line gives readers a landmark for when the two futures separate.
  • Region toggle: lets readers check whether California's gap differs from the global one (it ends larger: +3.92 °C vs +3.28 °C in 2081–2100).

Alternatives we considered

  • A map of California by grid cell: at about 1° resolution the state is only around 64 land cells, so a map adds little detail and makes comparing two futures over time harder.
  • A single-year slider: rejected because single years are dominated by natural variability (see exploratory graphs 2 and 4).
  • Two separate charts, one per scenario: rejected because comparing across charts is harder than comparing two lines on one axis.

Development process

  • Time spent: about 20 hours in total.
  • What took the most time: finding CMIP6 runs that matched across all three experiments and checking their units and year coverage, and then getting the brush, readouts and summary sentence to stay in sync.
