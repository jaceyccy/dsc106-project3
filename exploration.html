Two Futures, One Planet: How Our Emissions Choices Shape 2100

How does the projected temperature gap between a low-emissions and a high-emissions future change over the century?

Data: CMIP6 (Google Cloud public archive), model CESM2-WACCM, member r1i1p1f1, native grid (0.94° × 1.25°). Variable: monthly near-surface air temperature (tas), converted K → °C and averaged to calendar years (days-in-month weights). Region: California bounding box 32.5–42°N, 124.5–114°W, land cells only (land fraction ≥ 50 %, 64 of 88 grid cells), cos(latitude) area-weighted. The box also covers most of Nevada and slivers of Oregon, Arizona and Baja California. Scenarios: historical (1950–2014), SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 (2015–2100). One model run — not a multi-model ensemble.

1 · Annual temperature trajectories, 1950–2100

California box, annual mean °C. Gray: historical run; shaded: 1995–2014 baseline (mean 14.74 °C).

HistoricalSSP1-2.6 (low emissions)SSP5-8.5 (high emissions)

What we learned: The two futures are hard to tell apart until mid-century, then split. SSP1-2.6 levels off near 16.3 °C (about +0.08 °C per decade), while SSP5-8.5 keeps climbing (about +0.70 °C per decade) to 21.1 °C in 2100. This pointed us to the gap between scenarios, not either line alone.

2 · Annual gap: SSP5-8.5 minus SSP1-2.6

California box, °C, one bar per year 2015–2100. Orange = high-emissions year warmer; blue = low-emissions year warmer.

What we learned: The single-year gap is noisy. It is negative in 21 of 86 years (the last in 2059), then stays positive, reaching +5.26 °C in 2095. So year-by-year comparisons can mislead, and we needed a time-window view.

3 · Distribution of annual temperatures in a common window

California box, °C. Each dot is one year: 2081–2100 under each scenario, compared with the 1995–2014 baseline from the historical run.

Historical 1995–2014SSP1-2.6SSP5-8.5window mean

What we learned: By 2081–2100 the two scenarios don't overlap at all. SSP1-2.6's warmest year (17.07 °C) is still cooler than SSP5-8.5's coolest year (18.84 °C). SSP1-2.6 ends up only about 1.5 °C above the baseline, against about 5.5 °C for SSP5-8.5. Averaging over a window gives a clear contrast that single years hide.

4 · Year-to-year variation vs the smoothed trend

California box, °C, 2015–2100. Faint dots: individual years; lines: centered 11-year running mean (not shown for the first and last 5 years).

SSP1-2.6SSP5-8.5

What we learned: Natural variability around the 11-year trend is about 0.5–0.6 °C (SD). The smoothed gap passes +1 °C only in 2053 and +3 °C in 2079. Before mid-century the emissions signal is smaller than ordinary year-to-year swings, so our interaction should average over a viewer-chosen window, not show single years.

5 · Average scenario gap by 20-year window: California box vs global

Mean of annual gaps (SSP5-8.5 minus SSP1-2.6, °C) in each window. Same model run. Global = cos(latitude)-weighted mean over all grid cells.

California box (land)Global mean

What we learned: The gap doesn't grow at a steady rate. In California it goes from +0.24 °C (2021–2040) to +0.56 °C (2041–2060), +2.26 °C (2061–2080) and +3.92 °C (2081–2100), ending larger than the global gap (+3.28 °C). So the answer to our question depends on which window you pick, and that became our interaction: viewers choose the window.
